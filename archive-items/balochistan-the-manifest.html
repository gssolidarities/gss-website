THEMANIFEST

Balochistan is forty-four per cent of Pakistan's territory and roughly six per cent of its people. It supplied the gas that lit Multan and Karachi decades before it lit Quetta. It holds one of the largest undeveloped copper-gold deposits on earth and a deep-water port on the Arabian Sea. It sits at the bottom of nearly every human development table in the country. Enforced disappearance is defined in international law by exactly this absence: a deprivation of liberty followed by a refusal to acknowledge it, placing the person outside the protection of the law. Everything else on this page has paperwork. A gas field has a lease. A port has a concession. An operation has an order. The person has nothing — and the missing document is not an oversight. It is the instrument.

Curatorial statement

AN INTERNAL
COLONY HAS A LEDGER

Balochistan is routinely narrated as a security problem: a separatist insurgency, a counter-insurgency, an unfortunate cycle. That framing puts the violence first and the economics in the background, which is precisely backwards. The extraction is the older fact. Sui gas began flowing in the 1950s. The first operation was in 1948.

What makes this a decolonial archive rather than a South Asian politics page is that the pattern is not distinctive to Pakistan. It is the standard architecture of internal colonialism, and it is legible anywhere it appears: a resource-rich, sparsely-populated periphery; extraction under contracts negotiated elsewhere; local benefit deferred indefinitely; political demands answered as security threats; and a paramilitary force under central command exercising police powers over a population it does not come from. The same structure has run in the Niger Delta, in West Papua, in the Congolese copperbelt, in Bougainville.

So the page is a manifest. Everything that leaves has a document. Everything that arrives has a document. The one category that has no document is the person, and the collection is organised around the argument that this is a design property of the system and not a gap in its records.

The arithmetic that will not close

FOUR COUNTS.
NO RECONCILIATION.

Every serious body that has tried to count Balochistan's disappeared arrives at a different number. The archive's job is not to pick one. It is to explain why they cannot be made to agree — because the reason is itself the finding.

Commission of Inquiry on
Enforced Disappearances · Pakistan
10,636
cases received nationwide since 2011
8,986 disposed · 1,650 under investigation

A federal commission established in 2011. It counts complaints filed with it, and records a case as disposed when a person returns home, is located in an internment centre or a prison, or is found dead. Around 3,000 of the cases relate to Balochistan.

Event-based monitoring by an activist organisation. The Human Rights Council of Balochistan, counting independently, recorded 601 disappearances in 2023 and 830 in 2024. These are annual flows, not cumulative stocks — a different unit again.

WHY THEY DO NOT RECONCILE Four bodies are counting four different objects. The Commission counts complaints it received. Human Rights Watch counts the same complaints from outside. VBMP counts people, including those whose families never filed. Paank and HRCB count incidents in a year. None of these can be converted into the others without knowing the two things nobody knows: how many families never filed, and how many of the "disposed" cases were disposed of because a body was returned.

This is why the state's number is a defence of procedure rather than an answer. A commission that logs, traces and disposes generates an impressive audit trail while leaving the underlying question — who takes people, and under what authority — entirely untouched. In fourteen years no official has been prosecuted for an enforced disappearance in Balochistan.
Chronology

1839 —
PRESENT

Selected entries.

13 NOV 1839

The storming of Kalat

British forces take the fort at Kalat during the First Anglo-Afghan War; Mir Mehrab Khan is killed defending it. The Khanate is subsequently brought under British paramountcy, and from the 1870s administered through the Sandeman system of tribal intermediaries and subsidies.

11 AUG 1947

Kalat declares independence

The Khanate of Kalat declares itself independent, three days before the creation of Pakistan. Its two houses of parliament subsequently debate accession and decline it, favouring a treaty relationship instead.

27 MAR 1948

Accession

Following the accession of the subordinate states of Kharan, Las Bela and Makran and the movement of Pakistani troops, the Khan of Kalat signs an instrument of accession. His brother, Prince Abdul Karim, takes to the hills — the first of the insurgencies.

1952

Sui

Pakistan's largest natural gas field is discovered in Dera Bugti district. Gas is piped to Punjab and Sindh from the mid-1950s. Most of Balochistan, including districts adjacent to the field, waited decades for connection; large parts remain unconnected.

1958 · 1963–69

Second and third insurgencies

The One Unit scheme dissolving the provinces and the arrest of the Khan of Kalat trigger a rising under the octogenarian Nawab Nauroz Khan; he surrenders on assurances and his companions are hanged in 1960. A longer guerrilla campaign follows through the 1960s under Sher Muhammad Marri.

1973–1977

The fourth war

The federal government dismisses the elected National Awami Party provincial government. The resulting insurgency draws in tens of thousands of troops, supported at points by Iranian helicopters. Casualty estimates run into the thousands on both sides. It ends after the 1977 coup, with an amnesty.

2004–05

Sui, again

The alleged rape of Dr Shazia Khalid, a doctor at the Sui gas facility, and the handling of the case become a national controversy and a proximate trigger for renewed armed conflict in Dera Bugti. Attacks on gas infrastructure and military operations escalate together.

26 AUG 2006

Nawab Akbar Bugti is killed

The former governor and chief minister of Balochistan, then in his late seventies, is killed in a military operation in a cave complex in Kohlu district. His death converts a regional conflict into a generational one; recruitment to armed groups rises sharply afterwards.

2009 —

The camp outside the press club

After the abduction and killing of Baloch National Movement leader Ghulam Mohammad Baloch and two colleagues, families begin a protest camp outside the Quetta Press Club under the Voice for Baloch Missing Persons. It has now run for more than sixteen years and is among the longest continuous sit-ins anywhere in the world.

2011

"We Can Torture, Kill, or Keep You for Years"

Human Rights Watch publishes a report documenting enforced disappearances in Balochistan and the pattern that Baloch families call kill-and-dump: a person is taken, and weeks or months later a body is recovered bearing marks of custody, with no acknowledgement of detention.

2013–14

The long walk · and the graves at Tootak

Abdul Qadeer Baloch — Mama Qadeer — then in his seventies, walks from Quetta to Karachi and on to Islamabad, close to two thousand kilometres over four months, with Farzana Majeed Baloch and families of the missing. In January 2014 mass graves are found at Tootak in Khuzdar district; accounts of the number of bodies vary widely and no full identification followed.

2015 —

CPEC and Gwadar

The China–Pakistan Economic Corridor is formalised, with Gwadar as its maritime terminus. Local fishing communities protest restricted access to the sea, trawler licensing, water and electricity shortages and security fencing. The Gwadar Ko Haq Do movement of 2021–22 mobilises tens of thousands around these demands.

21 DEC 2020

Karima Baloch

The exiled activist and former chair of the Baloch Students Organisation–Azad is found dead in Toronto. Canadian police state they do not consider the death suspicious; her family and the Baloch movement reject that conclusion. Her death follows that of the journalist Sajid Hussain, found dead in Sweden earlier the same year.

DEC 2023

The Baloch Long March

The killing of Balaach Mola Bakhsh in Turbat in November 2023, days after his family said he had been taken by the Counter Terrorism Department, triggers a women-led march of roughly 1,600 kilometres from Kech to Islamabad. Marchers are met with water cannon and mass detention in the capital. Dr Mahrang Baloch emerges as its central figure.

MAR 2025

The Jaffar Express · and the crackdown

An armed group hijacks a passenger train in the Bolan pass, taking hundreds of civilians hostage; the siege ends with substantial loss of life. In the crackdown that follows, Baloch Yakjehti Committee leaders are detained after a Quetta sit-in at which the BYC says police fire killed three protesters, including a twelve-year-old. Mahrang Baloch is taken into custody on 22 March 2025.

JUN 2025

Section 11-EEEE

The Anti-Terrorism (Balochistan Amendment) Act inserts a preventive detention provision permitting a notified detaining authority — and, where the relevant section is invoked, the armed forces and intelligence agencies — to order detention without the ordinary judicial safeguards.

22 JUN 2026

Life imprisonment

An anti-terrorism court sentences Dr Mahrang Baloch and Sibghatullah Shahji to life imprisonment over the death of a paramilitary soldier at a July 2024 protest in Gwadar, on a case theory of incitement through speech. The trial is held inside Hudda Jail in Quetta with journalists and civil society excluded; both defendants boycotted it and deny the charges. In August 2026 a petition is filed with the UN Working Group on Arbitrary Detention.

Method & ethics

WHAT THIS
ARCHIVE REFUSES

Standing rules for Collection 08

1. No images of returned bodies. Photographs of mutilated remains circulate widely in Baloch advocacy, and families have their own reasons for circulating them. This archive is not the family, and reproducing them here would make a spectacle of what is, for those families, evidence.

2. Counts are always attributed to the counter. No figure in this collection appears without the body that produced it and the unit it measures. Where numbers conflict, the conflict is displayed rather than resolved.

3. Attacks on civilians by armed groups are violations, not context. The Jaffar Express hijacking, the killing of bus passengers after identity checks, and attacks on coal miners and teachers are entries in this archive on the same footing as state violence. A liberation claim does not license a hostage-taking, and an archive that treats one as background to the other is producing propaganda.

4. We do not adjudicate independence. The Baloch national question is for Baloch people to settle. This collection documents enforced disappearance, extraction without consent, and the criminalisation of peaceful assembly — none of which becomes acceptable under any answer to the sovereignty question.

5. Sources at risk are protected. Activists in Balochistan and in the diaspora have been killed. Where a source has chosen publicity — as the BYC and VBMP have, deliberately and at cost — we name them. Where a source has not, we do not, and we do not aggregate details that would identify them.
