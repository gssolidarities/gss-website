THE ISNREGISTRY

On arrival at Guantánamo Bay a man stopped being a name and became an Internment Serial Number. The number was the point. A number can be transferred, cleared, reviewed, and held for twenty-four years without anyone having to say a name out loud in a courtroom. This collection takes the number as its unit — and then puts the names back beside it.

Curatorial statement

A PLACE BUILT
OUT OF A LOOPHOLE

Guantánamo is usually narrated as an excess — a policy that went too far after a catastrophe. That story is wrong in a way that matters. Guantánamo was not an overreaction. It was a site selection.

The 1903 lease gives Cuba "ultimate sovereignty" and the United States "complete jurisdiction and control." For a century that clause was a curiosity. In late 2001 it became an engineering specification: lawyers were asked to find territory where American power operated fully and American law did not, and the lease provided it. The base was chosen precisely because of the sentence in the treaty.

This is why the collection belongs in a decolonial archive rather than a counter-terrorism one. The instrument that produced Guantánamo is a colonial-era lease extracted from an occupied Cuba in 1903. Guantánamo does not represent the suspension of the rule of law. It represents the ordinary functioning of a legal order that has always maintained zones where its own rules are held not to apply — the plantation, the reservation, the protectorate, the camp.

The architecture

FIVE LOAD-BEARING
DEVICES

Guantánamo is a legal structure before it is a physical one. These are the components that hold it up. Remove any one and the building does not stand.

DEVICE 01

The Lease

The 1903 Cuban–American Treaty, renewed 1934, splits sovereignty from control. Cuba is sovereign; the United States governs. The split is the whole trick: it lets the government argue that constitutional protections tied to US soil do not attach, while its own authority remains total.

DEVICE 02

The Category

"Unlawful enemy combatant" — a status invented to sit between prisoner of war and criminal defendant, so that neither the Geneva Conventions' POW protections nor ordinary criminal due process would apply. In February 2002 the President determined Geneva did not apply to al-Qaeda and Taliban detainees.

DEVICE 03

The Number

The Internment Serial Number replaced the name in every operational document. For years the government resisted publishing the list of who was held. The number made a person administrable and unfindable at once — a bureaucratic form of disappearance.

DEVICE 04

The Commission

Military commissions, established 2001 and reconstituted by statute in 2006 and 2009, operate with different evidentiary rules from federal courts. Cases against the highest-profile defendants have run for well over a decade without reaching trial, largely because the evidence is entangled with torture.

DEVICE 05

The Ban on Exit

Congress has repeatedly prohibited transferring detainees to the United States for any purpose, including trial and medical care, through annual defence authorisation legislation. Two presidents ordered closure. The prohibition, not the executive order, is what has held.

Chronology

TWENTY-FOUR
YEARS

Selected entries. Dates are given as recorded in the public documentary record.

1903 · Feb

The lease is signed

The Cuban–American Treaty grants the United States a coaling and naval station at Guantánamo Bay, reserving ultimate sovereignty to Cuba while conferring complete jurisdiction and control on the United States. Confirmed by a further treaty in 1934, terminable only by mutual agreement.

1991–94

The Haitian precedent

Tens of thousands of Haitian and Cuban asylum seekers interdicted at sea are held at the base. Litigation over their treatment — including the detention of HIV-positive Haitians — establishes the pattern of the base as a place for people the United States wishes to hold outside its own law.

2002 · 11 Jan

Camp X-Ray opens

The first twenty detainees arrive from Afghanistan. Photographs of men in orange jumpsuits, goggled and kneeling in open wire cages, are released by the Department of Defense itself as a demonstration of resolve.

2002 · 7 Feb

Geneva declared inapplicable

Presidential determination that the Third Geneva Convention does not apply to al-Qaeda detainees and that Taliban detainees are unlawful combatants not entitled to POW status.

2002 · 1 Aug

The Bybee memo

Office of Legal Counsel opinion narrowing the definition of torture to pain equivalent to organ failure or death, and asserting that the President's commander-in-chief authority could override the federal torture statute. Withdrawn in 2004.

2004 · 28 Jun

Rasul v. Bush

The Supreme Court holds that US courts have jurisdiction to hear habeas corpus petitions from Guantánamo detainees. The government responds by creating Combatant Status Review Tribunals.

2006 · 29 Jun

Hamdan v. Rumsfeld

The Supreme Court strikes down the original military commissions and holds that Common Article 3 of the Geneva Conventions applies. Congress responds within months with the Military Commissions Act of 2006, which strips habeas jurisdiction.

2006 · 10 Jun

Three deaths in one night

Yasser Talal al-Zahrani, Mani Shaman al-Utaybi and Ali Abdullah Ahmed are found dead in their cells. The camp commander describes the deaths publicly as asymmetric warfare against the United States. Independent investigations have contested the official account.

2006 · Sept

Black sites emptied into Guantánamo

Fourteen "high-value detainees" previously held in the CIA's secret overseas detention programme — including Khalid Sheikh Mohammed, Ammar al-Baluchi, Hambali and Abu Zubaydah — are transferred to Guantánamo. Their torture, occurring before arrival, becomes the permanent obstacle to trying them.

2008 · 12 Jun

Boumediene v. Bush

The Supreme Court holds that the constitutional right to habeas corpus extends to Guantánamo and that the Military Commissions Act's suspension of it is unconstitutional. The DC Circuit's subsequent jurisprudence sharply narrows the practical effect.

2009 · 22 Jan

Executive Order 13492

President Obama orders closure within one year. Congress subsequently bars transfers of detainees into the United States in successive defence authorisation acts. The prison does not close.

2013

Mass hunger strike

A camp-wide hunger strike over conditions and indefinite detention draws force-feeding by nasogastric tube. The World Medical Association and the American Medical Association hold that force-feeding a competent hunger striker violates medical ethics.

2014 · 9 Dec

The Senate torture study

The Senate Select Committee on Intelligence releases the 525-page executive summary of its study of the CIA detention and interrogation programme. The full report — over six thousand pages — remains classified.

2021–25

The slow drain

Transfers resume. In January 2025 eleven Yemeni men are transferred to Oman, the largest single transfer in years, leaving fifteen. Of these, some are cleared for transfer, some are before the commissions, and two have been convicted.

2025 · 29 Jan

The base is repurposed

A presidential memorandum orders expansion of the Migrant Operations Center on the leeward side of the base to hold up to 30,000 people facing deportation. The first flight arrives 4 February 2025. Litigation follows over denial of access to counsel; the tent facility is found not to meet detention standards and detainees are moved to Louisiana.

2026 · 11 Jan

Year twenty-five

The prison enters its twenty-fifth year of operation with fifteen men held. Every one of them has been in custody for more than a decade.

Detainee population at Guantánamo, approximate figures at key points. The curve of this chart is the entire political history of the prison: a rapid fill, a long refusal to empty, and a residue that no administration has been willing or able to release.

Two facts sit behind every number above. First: the overwhelming majority of the 780 were never charged with any offence, and hundreds were transferred to the United States by bounty rather than captured by American forces on a battlefield. Second: for most of the prison's history the largest single category of detainee was not "convicted", not "charged", but cleared for release and still inside.

Method & ethics

WHAT THIS
ARCHIVE REFUSES

Standing rules for Collection 05

1. No trophy imagery. The 2002 photographs of kneeling, goggled men in orange were released by the Department of Defense as a display of power. We describe them; we do not reproduce them. An image circulated by the captor to demonstrate domination does not become evidence against him by being reprinted.

2. The number is never used alone. Where an ISN appears in this collection it appears attached to a name. The registry is adopted in order to be broken.

3. Torture-derived statements are not evidence. Where the record contains admissions or accusations obtained under the CIA programme or at Guantánamo, we do not repeat their content as fact about any individual. We record that they exist and how they were obtained.

4. Neither innocence nor guilt is the frame. The objection to indefinite detention without charge does not depend on the detainee being innocent. Framing the archive around wrongly-held men concedes that rightly-held men may be held this way.

5. This is not a closed file. Fifteen men are inside as this page is served.
