THE PAPERTRAIL

Abu Ghraib is filed in public memory as a photography scandal — a few soldiers, a digital camera, a night shift out of control. That filing is itself the cover-up. This collection contains no photographs. It follows the memoranda instead, because the memoranda are where the decisions were made, and because the decisions were made in writing, by people who signed their names.

Exhibit A · reading exercise CLICK ANY BLACK BAR TO LIFT IT
The abuses at Abu Ghraib were carried out by a small number of soldiers acting on their own initiative. Investigators later established that interrogation techniques had been approved in writing at the level of the Secretary of Defense, migrated from Guantánamo to Iraq by the officer who had commanded the Guantánamo camp, sent to Baghdad to "GTMOize" operations, and applied in a facility where the International Committee of the Red Cross estimated that between 70 and 90 per cent of those detained had been arrested by mistake. Of everyone in the chain of command, the number of officers above the rank of lieutenant colonel who faced criminal conviction was zero.
Every lifted phrase is drawn from the public investigative record. The bars are ours — placed to make visible what the official narrative kept under one.

Curatorial statement

WHY THERE ARE
NO PHOTOGRAPHS HERE

The images from Abu Ghraib did the work of exposure. They also did other work. They produced a story with a cast of five or six low-ranking soldiers and a moral centre located in a night shift on Tier 1A. They gave the public a face to be disgusted by, and the faces available were a reservist from West Virginia and a twenty-year-old woman from a trailer park in Fort Ashby.

Meanwhile the photographs did something worse to the men in them. They were made naked and posed, and then the record of that posing was circulated globally, indefinitely, in newspapers and textbooks and now on every archive page that reproduces them. The men were stripped once by the guards and a second time by the world's press. Nearly all of them have never been named. To reprint the images is to keep them permanently in that state, for a pedagogical purpose they never consented to.

So this collection refuses them. What it holds instead is the paper: the legal opinions, the signed authorisations, the transfer of technique from Guantánamo to Baghdad, the investigations that named the practice and stopped at the rank of colonel. The paper is more incriminating than the photographs, and it implicates people who were never photographed.

The chain of authorisation

TWELVE
DOCUMENTS

Read downward. Each document is real, dated, and attributable. The question this sequence answers is not whether torture occurred at Abu Ghraib — that is settled — but where the decision to permit it was taken, and by whom.

Outcome by rank

WHERE THE
LADDER STOPS

Criminal accountability for Abu Ghraib mapped against rank and role. The pattern is not a coincidence of individual cases; it is the shape the accountability process was permitted to take.

Cabinet level
Secretary of Defense
No charges
Signed the December 2002 authorisation of counter-resistance techniques for Guantánamo, later rescinded. Remained in office until 2006.
Office of Legal Counsel
Justice Department lawyers
No charges
Authors of the August 2002 torture memoranda. A DOJ Office of Professional Responsibility finding of professional misconduct was downgraded on review to poor judgment. No bar sanction, no prosecution.
General officers
Commanders, CJTF-7 and JTF-GTMO
No charges
The officer who had commanded Guantánamo was sent to Iraq in August 2003 to assess and improve intelligence yield. No general officer faced criminal charges arising from Abu Ghraib.
Brigadier General
Commander, 800th MP Brigade
Demoted
Janis Karpinski was relieved, reprimanded and demoted to colonel — the most senior officer punished, and punished administratively rather than criminally.
Lieutenant Colonel
Director, Joint Interrogation and Debriefing Center
Reprimand
Steven Jordan was the only officer court-martialed. The abuse-related charges were dismissed; his sole conviction, for disobeying an order, was later overturned and replaced by a reprimand.
Enlisted soldiers
372nd Military Police Company and others
Prison
Eleven soldiers were convicted. Charles Graner received ten years; Ivan Frederick eight; Lynndie England three. All were of rank specialist, sergeant or staff sergeant.
Private contractors
Interrogators and translators
Civil liability
No contractor employee was criminally prosecuted. In November 2024 a civil jury found CACI Premier Technology liable for conspiracy to commit torture; the Fourth Circuit affirmed the $42 million award in March 2026 — twenty-two years after the events.
The detained
Thousands of Iraqi civilians
No process
The ICRC estimated in early 2004 that 70 to 90 per cent of those detained in Iraq had been arrested by mistake. Almost none received a hearing, compensation, or an apology.

Testimony

WHO IS ALLOWED
TO SPEAK

The soldiers gave interviews, wrote books, appeared in documentaries. Almost none of the men in the photographs were ever named in the Western press. Three of them reached a courtroom in 2024, after sixteen years of litigation.

Sixteen years of litigation produced the first American jury ever to hear a survivor of post-9/11 US torture describe what was done to him.

Method & ethics

WHAT THIS
ARCHIVE REFUSES

Standing rules for Collection 06

1. No photographs of detainees. Not thumbnailed, not blurred, not "for context." The men in those images were never asked and cannot now be asked. Their nakedness is not a teaching aid.

2. No descriptive re-enactment. We name the categories of abuse established by the official investigations. We do not narrate individual acts in detail. Detailed description of sexual humiliation reproduces the humiliation in prose and serves no evidentiary purpose the finding does not already serve.

3. Names travel upward. Where an enlisted soldier is named, an officer, a lawyer or a cabinet member is named in the same entry. An archive that names only the convicted reproduces the prosecution's own selection.

4. Survivors are named where they have chosen to be named. The Al Shimari plaintiffs chose a public courtroom. Others did not, and are not identified here.

5. The word is torture. Not abuse, not mistreatment, not detainee-handling irregularities. The euphemism was itself part of the apparatus, and a counter-archive that adopts it has already lost the argument.
