The Planetary Justice Map

Global South Solidarities Anarchive · open source · evidence-first

This is a living, citation-grounded map of radical solidarities across borders: revolutionary figures and movements of the Global Souths, the trajectories their lives cut across the world — birth, exile, organising, return — and the documented ties between them. It is a research instrument and a teaching instrument, built to be read closely.

How to read it

  • Nodes are figures (filled points) and movements (rings), coloured by thread.
  • Dashed arcs trace a node's real trajectory through the world; gradient arcs are documented solidarity ties between nodes.
  • Click any node — on the map or in the constellation index — for its dossier: a factual annotation, an explicitly-labelled interpretive reading, every place with its coordinates and precision class, and named sources with stable evidence codes.
  • Scrub or play the timeline to watch the network assemble across a century.
  • Open the STORY MAPS — three interactive storytelling maps, one for each of the project's research questions, argued over the real geography with verbatim primary voices.
  • Concepts are nodes too (diamond glyphs): theory with a coinage site, a date and a genealogy — superexploitation, two colonialisms, jineolojî, Kawsak Sacha, the Third World, clandestinity — tied to the figures and movements that made them.
  • Lenses re-render the same data as argument: source geography colours each node by where its record is held (watch the archive's centre of gravity), and care infrastructure foregrounds the schools, clinics, presses and apartments — a map of infrastructure, not heroes.
  • PATHWAYS are guided teaching routes with UG/GRAD prompts; TEACH holds the course kit, syllabus shapes and the node-proposal assignment.

Standards

No node without named institutional or scholarly sources. Factual annotation and interpretive reading are kept apart and labelled. Area-level coordinates are flagged APPROX and must not be read as precise. Ties appear only where documented — or where the project's research design itself pairs nodes, in which case the tie says so. Primary materials — photographs, speeches, writings, records — are linked at their holding archives, never re-hosted; rights remain with their holders. The dataset is a versioned file; every change is auditable.

Interactive Storytelling Maps

three research questions, argued in space and time

Each story map is a scroll-driven, interactive investigation of one of the project's research questions: camera flights over the real geography, verbatim primary voices, coded evidence, and a clearly-labelled interpretive reading at the end. Around ten minutes each.

Teach with the map

course-ready · open access · citable

The map is built for classrooms: every claim is sourced, every pin carries a precision class, factual annotation is kept apart from labelled interpretation, and every node, story map and scene has a stable link that fits on a syllabus.

A first session (15–20 min)

Press play on the timeline and watch the century assemble. Open one node dossier and read its annotation against its reading — ask what changed between the two. Then walk one story map together, stopping at the primary voices.

Discussion prompts

  • Claudia Jones theorised “superexploitation” in 1949 — four decades before intersectionality entered the lexicon. What is gained, and what is lost, when a movement concept becomes a classroom vocabulary?
  • RAWA's politics survived by going clandestine — schools, a magazine, hidden cameras. Where does politics happen when public politics is forbidden, and who does that work?
  • The PAIGC wrote women into liberated-zone governance by quota; Guinea-Bissau's National Women's Day commemorates a fallen woman commander. Is this feminism inside revolution, or revolution inside feminism (Urdang's “two colonialisms”)?
  • What does the word mother do politically in Lorena Borjas's story — and what does it hide about the labour of community defence?
  • Nenquimo and Gualinga are two generations from neighbouring nations. How does their pairing change the picture of “leadership” that climate politics usually rewards?
  • The TWLF was a coalition of coalitions. What made that form durable enough to win an institution — and what tensions does the map's tie-drawing rule (documented ties only) make visible by leaving lines out?

Assignment frameworks

  • Deepen a node. Choose one figure or movement; verify one “pending verification” citation to its precise URL; draft a 150-word annotation upgrade with sources, following the METHOD panel's standards. (Research methods · archives)
  • Argue a new node. Propose one figure or movement the map should add. The proposal must satisfy the roster-integrity rule: named institutional/scholarly sources, places with honest precision classes, an annotation kept apart from a reading. (Historiography · WGSS theory)
  • Test a story map. Take one story map's closing reading and test its model against a node it was not written about — where does the model hold, where does it break? (Comparative analysis · theory-building)

Pathways — guided routes with prompts

Four authored pathways walk the map stop by stop, each stop a live view plus a framing paragraph and a prompt — with an UG / GRAD register toggle: same stops, different questions. Orientation (how to read a claim) · Conceptual (superexploitation before intersectionality) · Comparative (motherhood as political grammar) · Adversarial (read the map against itself). Progress saves in your browser; every pathway has a syllabus-safe URL.

Syllabus shapes

Three ready-to-adapt course skeletons, print-friendly: 3-week unit · 8-week half-semester · 15-week standalone.

Citing

Use CITE for Chicago/BibTeX records of the map; each story map has its own CITE button. Node anchors (#claudia-jones) and scene anchors cite specific views, and COPY VIEW captures filters, timeline year and lenses as one URL. The dataset is a versioned file — quote it by version (see METHOD).
